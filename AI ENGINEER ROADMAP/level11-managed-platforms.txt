Level 11 · Managed AI Platforms

Goal: Ship an AI application on the platforms teams actually buy — a cloud provider's managed platform, an open-weight hosting provider, or a router spanning several — and know which parts to hand over and which to keep.
Prerequisites: Level 9 (system design), Level 10 (LLMOps) · Depth: Conceptual + hands-on; cloud-account access required · Suggested duration: 18 sessions.

M11.1 · The three ways to consume a model — (sessions: 1)

Understand the three paths an organization can take: a provider's public API, a managed platform inside its own cloud account, or open weights it hosts itself or through an inference provider.
Recognize that open weights no longer imply operating GPUs, since managed platforms and inference providers serve the same open models on demand.
Understand why most regulated organizations cannot use a public API directly, and why that constraint decides architecture long before quality does.
Compare the three paths on identity, data handling, region, capacity, price per token, operational effort, and how quickly new models reach you.
Understand where a managed platform sits relative to the model gateway pattern: it can be your gateway, sit behind one, or be one provider among several that a gateway routes to.
Try: Take an application you have already built against a public API and list every component that would change if the same app had to run inside a cloud account.
Mental model: One application, three consumption paths → public API · managed platform in your cloud · open weights you or a provider host
Outcome: Place any AI workload on the right consumption path and defend the choice with reasons other than familiarity.

M11.2 · Platform anatomy: Bedrock, Vertex AI & Azure AI Foundry — (sessions: 2)

Session 1 · What a managed platform gives you
Understand the shape all three share: a catalog of closed and open-weight models, one endpoint style, one identity system, one network boundary, and one bill.
Read a platform's model catalog the way you read a model card, noting which providers are offered, which regions carry them, and which versions are pinned or retired.
Understand how invocation differs from a provider SDK, including the platform's own request format, its converse-style unified API, and the provider-native passthrough where one exists.
Recognize the platform-specific vocabulary for the same ideas, so a Bedrock, Vertex AI, or Azure AI Foundry document reads as a variation rather than a new subject.
Session 2 · Working on one platform
Call a hosted model on one platform from application code and confirm the response, usage, and cost reporting you get back.
Compare the same prompt across two models in the same catalog without changing how you authenticate, network, or bill.
Understand what the platform does not give you: prompt quality, retrieval design, evaluation depth, and product behavior remain yours.
Try: Run one prompt against a closed model and an open-weight model in the same catalog, and compare quality, latency, and price per thousand tokens.
Outcome: Work confidently on at least one managed platform and map its concepts onto the other two.

M11.3 · Identity, networking & tenancy — (sessions: 2)

Session 1 · Identity and access
Replace API keys with cloud identity: roles, service accounts, and workload identity that grant a service the right to invoke a model.
Apply least privilege to model access, scoping which models, regions, and actions an application or team may use.
Understand how platform access is audited, and why an audit trail of who invoked which model matters in regulated environments.
Session 2 · Network boundary and tenancy
Reach a model over private connectivity so requests never traverse the public internet, and understand what that buys you and what it costs.
Design tenant isolation on a shared platform: separate accounts or projects, scoped credentials, per-tenant keys, and retrieval that cannot cross a tenant boundary.
Understand encryption and key management options, including customer-managed keys for data at rest.
Try: Invoke a model from a private network path with no public egress, then verify from the logs that the request never left the account.
Outcome: Deploy an AI workload whose identity, network path, and tenant boundaries would survive a security review.

M11.4 · Regions, residency & compliance — (sessions: 1)

Understand that a model exists in specific regions, and that the region you are permitted to use often decides which models you may design around.
Plan for data residency requirements, including Indian data-localization expectations and the sector rules that sit above them.
Understand retention and training terms in writing: what the platform logs, for how long, who can see it, and whether your data may be used to improve a model.
Connect these controls to the governance work of Level 8, where residency, deletion, and third-party processing were named as requirements.
Handle the common case where the model you want is unavailable in your required region, through cross-region reasoning, a different model, or an exception you can document.
Outcome: State, for a given workload, exactly where the data goes, where it is processed, how long it is kept, and under whose terms.

M11.5 · Capacity, quotas & cost — (sessions: 2)

Session 1 · Capacity
Distinguish on-demand capacity from provisioned or reserved throughput, and understand why committed capacity changes both price and rate limits.
Read the quota model of a platform: requests per minute, tokens per minute, concurrent requests, and per-model limits that differ by region.
Plan for burst traffic with queueing, backpressure, and batch endpoints instead of assuming the platform will absorb a spike.
Request and defend a quota increase with traffic evidence, since capacity is an approval process rather than a setting.
Session 2 · Cost
Model cost per request end to end, including input and output tokens, retrieval calls, and any managed component you invoke along the way.
Attribute cost to a project, team, or tenant using the platform's own tagging and billing views, which a shared API key cannot do.
Set budgets and alerts so a runaway agent loop is caught by spend monitoring rather than by the monthly invoice.
Compare on-demand, committed, and self-hosted economics at your actual volume rather than at the volume in the pricing example.
Try: Take a week of real traffic and price it three ways: on-demand, committed capacity, and open weights on your own inference.
Outcome: Size capacity and forecast spend for a real workload, and know at what volume each pricing model wins.

M11.6 · Open-weight model platforms — (sessions: 2)

Session 1 · Where open weights actually run
Understand the spectrum between calling an API and operating hardware: model hubs, serverless inference providers, GPU compute platforms, and your own servers.
Use a model hub such as Hugging Face to find open-weight models, read their cards, check licences and gated access, and judge whether a checkpoint is trustworthy.
Call an open-weight model through a serverless inference provider such as Together, Fireworks, Groq, or Replicate, where you pay per token and never see a GPU.
Understand what a GPU compute platform such as Modal, Baseten, or RunPod adds: you bring the serving stack and they schedule, scale, and bill the hardware.
Serve a model yourself with vLLM, TGI, or Ollama, and understand continuous batching, KV-cache reuse, and why a naive request loop wastes most of a GPU.
Session 2 · Choosing and paying for open-weight serving
Compare per-token pricing with per-GPU-hour pricing, and find the utilization point at which renting hardware becomes cheaper than paying per token.
Understand cold starts and scale-to-zero, and why a deployment that idles cheaply may answer its first request slowly.
Size hardware for a model from parameter count, precision, quantization, and KV-cache growth with context length, since memory decides which GPU you need.
Understand what hosting open weights gives you — predictable cost at volume, data that never leaves your boundary, and full control of versions — and what it asks of you in return.
Evaluate an open-weight model against a closed one on your own task before assuming that either is obviously better.
Try: Serve one small open-weight model three ways — a serverless provider, a GPU platform, and locally — and compare setup effort, latency, and the cost of a thousand requests.
Mental model: Open weights are a ladder: someone else's API → someone else's GPUs → your GPUs
Outcome: Run an open-weight model on the right rung of the hosting ladder and justify the choice on cost, latency, control, and effort.

M11.7 · Model routers & LLM gateways — (sessions: 2)

Session 1 · What a router is for
Understand a gateway as one endpoint in front of many providers: it holds the credentials, accepts one request format, and hides which model actually served the request.
Compare hosted routers such as OpenRouter and Portkey with self-hosted proxies such as LiteLLM, and with the routing built into cloud platforms and framework gateways.
Route by rule — task type, cost ceiling, context length, latency target, region, or risk — and fail over automatically when a provider errors or throttles.
Use a gateway's spend controls: virtual keys per team or per feature, budgets, quotas, and per-key rate limits that stop one client exhausting shared capacity.
Get one view of usage, latency, errors, and cost across every provider instead of stitching separate dashboards together.
Session 2 · Operating a router
Understand the failure mode you have introduced, since a gateway now sits on the critical path and needs its own availability, timeouts, and a bypass plan.
Handle the provider differences a gateway cannot hide: tool-calling formats, structured-output support, token limits, and prompts that behave differently on each model.
Cache at the gateway, both exact-match and semantic, and decide which requests must never be answered from a cache.
Decide when a router earns its place — several providers, several teams, or spend worth governing — and when one provider and a client library is the honest answer.
Keep evals in the loop, because a routing rule change swaps the model answering your users without a single line of application code changing.
Try: Put a router in front of two providers, force the primary to fail, and confirm the fallback answers inside your latency budget.
Outcome: Run model traffic through a router that gives you failover, spend control, and one view of usage, without pretending that providers are interchangeable.

M11.8 · Managed building blocks: build or buy — (sessions: 3)

Session 1 · Retrieval and agents
Understand managed retrieval offerings such as knowledge bases and enterprise search, which package ingestion, chunking, embedding, and retrieval behind a configuration.
Compare a managed knowledge base with the retrieval stack you built in Level 4 on control over chunking, retrieval tuning, evaluation depth, and cost at your corpus size.
Understand managed agent and workflow runtimes, and what they assume about tools, memory, and orchestration compared with the agents you built in Level 5.
Session 2 · Safety, evaluation and adaptation
Understand managed guardrails for content filtering, topic restriction, and sensitive-data redaction, and where they sit relative to your own checks.
Understand managed evaluation offerings and judge whether their metrics answer the questions your eval suite from Level 7 was built to answer.
Understand managed fine-tuning and distillation, including which base models can be adapted, where the resulting weights live, and whether you can take them with you.
Session 3 · Deciding, layer by layer
Decide each layer separately, since adopting a managed retrieval service does not commit you to managed agents, guardrails, or evaluation.
Judge each candidate on control, transparency, evaluation depth, cost at your volume, and how much of the behavior you must be able to explain to an auditor.
Measure the portability cost before adopting: a prompt moves between providers in minutes, while a knowledge base, agent, or guardrail configuration usually does not.
Recognize the honest cases for buying: a small team, a standard use case, a compliance deadline, or a workload whose quality bar the managed component already clears.
Try: Rebuild one component of an existing application on a managed equivalent, then compare quality, cost, and how much control you gave up.
Mental model: Buy the plumbing you cannot differentiate on, build the layer your product is judged by
Outcome: Make a defensible build-or-buy call for every layer of an AI system instead of adopting or rejecting managed services wholesale.

M11.9 · Portability, lock-in & migration — (sessions: 2)

Session 1 · Designing for exit
Separate what is portable from what is not: prompts, evals, and application code travel easily, while managed indexes, agent definitions, and platform-specific APIs do not.
Keep an abstraction between application code and provider so a model swap is a configuration change rather than a rewrite.
Understand the real costs of lock-in beyond price: model availability, feature pace, negotiating position, and the work of proving a new platform is safe.
Session 2 · Moving
Plan a migration between platforms or providers as an evaluation exercise, re-running your eval suite before, during, and after the move.
Handle catalog lag and platform-side deprecation, since managed catalogs trail the frontier and retire versions on their own schedule.
Run two platforms in parallel where the workload justifies it, using a gateway to route by capability, region, cost, or availability.
Understand multi-cloud honestly: it buys resilience and leverage, and it costs duplicated evaluation, duplicated security review, and duplicated operational knowledge.
Outcome: Adopt a platform without being trapped by it, and move a workload between platforms without discovering the coupling too late.

M11.10 · Capstone: deploy on a managed platform — (sessions: 1)

Take an application you built in an earlier level and redeploy it against a managed platform, keeping the behavior and changing the plumbing.
Authenticate through cloud identity with least-privilege access to a named set of models, and remove the provider API key from the application entirely.
Route model traffic over a private network path, and record where the data is processed, how long it is retained, and under which terms.
Replace at least one hand-built component with a managed equivalent, and keep at least one hand-built because you can defend why.
Re-run your evaluation suite on the migrated system and report what changed in quality, latency, and cost.
Route the application's model calls through a gateway with a working fallback, or state why direct calls to one provider are enough at your scale.
Size capacity for your expected traffic, set a budget alert, and state the volume at which a different pricing model would win.
Write a one-page platform decision record: the workload, the constraints, the path chosen, the components bought and built, and the exit plan.
Final result: the same application, running where a regulated employer could actually run it, with the identity, network, residency, capacity, cost, and portability decisions written down.
Level 11 completion outcome

By the end of this level, you should be able to:

Place a workload on the right consumption path among a public API, a managed platform, and open weights you or a provider host.
Work on at least one managed platform and read the other two as variations rather than new subjects.
Replace API keys with cloud identity and scope model access with least privilege.
Reach a model over a private network path and explain what that guarantees.
Isolate tenants on shared infrastructure, including retrieval that cannot cross a tenant boundary.
State where data is processed, how long it is retained, and whether it may be used for training.
Plan around regional model availability and data-residency requirements.
Size on-demand versus provisioned capacity and defend a quota increase with traffic evidence.
Attribute spend per project, team, or tenant, and catch runaway cost with budgets and alerts.
Place an open-weight model on the right rung of the hosting ladder, from a serverless provider to your own GPUs.
Compare per-token and per-GPU-hour economics and find the volume at which each one wins.
Run model traffic through a router with failover, spend controls, and one view of usage across providers.
Decide build or buy for retrieval, agents, guardrails, evaluation, and fine-tuning, layer by layer.
Measure the portability cost of a managed component before adopting it.
Migrate a workload between platforms with your eval suite as the safety net.
Deploy an existing application inside a cloud account in a form a regulated employer could accept.
