Level 12 · Multimodal 101: Beyond Text

Goal: Build a strong mental model of how models handle images, audio, and video, why they fail differently from text models, and how to choose and use the right model when a task stops being text-only.
Prerequisites: Level 1 (LLM 101), Level 2 (orchestration), Level 4 (RAG) · Depth: Conceptual + hands-on; no research papers or heavy mathematics · Suggested duration: 20 sessions.

M12.1 · Beyond text: what a multimodal model is — (sessions: 1)

Understand what makes a model multimodal: images, audio, and video are converted into tokens and fed into the same transformer that already processes text.
Distinguish a natively multimodal model, trained on mixed data from the start, from a pipeline that bolts a separate transcription, OCR, or captioning model in front of a text-only LLM.
Understand why text remains the interlingua: whatever enters the model, the reasoning and almost always the output happen in tokens that behave like text.
Build intuition for what changes and what does not: the context window, tokenization, sampling, and failure modes you learned for text all still apply.
Recognize what "any-to-any" marketing does and does not mean today, since most models accept several input modalities but produce only text.
Understand why a bolted-on pipeline loses information that a native model keeps, such as tone in speech or layout in a scanned page.
Try: Send the same chart as an image to a natively multimodal model and as OCR-extracted text to a text-only model, then compare what each one can answer.
Mental model: Image / audio / video → tokens → the same transformer you already understand → text out
Outcome: Explain to someone what a multimodal model actually is, and tell whether a given product is using a native model or a pipeline stitched together around a text model.

M12.2 · Vision: how images become tokens — (sessions: 1)

Understand how a vision encoder splits an image into a grid of patches and turns each patch into a vector the transformer can consume.
Build intuition for why a single image can cost hundreds or thousands of tokens, and why that cost scales with resolution rather than with file size.
Understand tiling and detail settings: high-detail modes slice a large image into multiple tiles, each priced as its own set of tokens.
Recognize what downscaling and aspect-ratio changes destroy, especially small text, thin lines, dense tables, and fine chart labels.
Understand that image tokens occupy the same context window as text tokens, so a handful of screenshots can crowd out the instructions and history around them.
Understand the practical trade-off between sending one large detailed image, several cropped regions, or a downscaled overview.
Try: Send the same screenshot at full resolution, at half resolution, and cropped to the region that matters, then compare token usage, cost, and answer quality.
Outcome: Predict roughly what an image will cost in tokens and choose a resolution and cropping strategy instead of uploading whatever the camera produced.

M12.3 · Vision: capabilities & failure modes — (sessions: 1)

Understand what vision models genuinely do well: reading text in images, describing scenes, extracting named fields from documents, and interpreting charts at a coarse level.
Understand the failure modes that repeat across models: precise counting, spatial and geometric reasoning, dense small text, and exact row-to-column alignment in tables.
Recognize fabricated detail, where a model confidently reports values, labels, or objects that a low-resolution or ambiguous image never actually contained.
Understand why these failures happen: the model is generating a plausible description of an image it perceives as patches, not measuring or verifying anything.
Adopt the engineering question for vision too: not "can this model read invoices?", but "how reliably does it read my invoices, at my resolution, in my formats?".
Understand which safeguards apply when reliability is not enough, such as higher resolution, cropping, structured output, verification passes, or human review.
Try: Build ten test images that include a crowded shelf, a dense table, a low-resolution receipt, and a handwritten note, then score how often the model is exactly right.
Outcome: Predict which visual tasks a model will handle unaided and which need cropping, higher resolution, or a verification step before you can ship them.

M12.4 · Vision: documents, charts & visual RAG — (sessions: 3)

Session 1 · Reading a document as an image
Distinguish a digital PDF that carries its own text layer from a scan or photograph where every character has to be recovered from pixels.
Compare OCR-based reading with native vision reading by a multimodal model on accuracy, cost, latency, and how much layout they preserve.
Understand why reading order matters: multi-column pages, headers, footers, footnotes, and sidebars can turn a correct page into nonsense when flattened badly.
Read tables and forms reliably, including merged cells, multi-page tables, checkboxes, and key-value layouts where the label and the value sit far apart.
Understand what a model actually does with a chart: it reads axes, labels, and legends rather than the underlying numbers, so precise values are often estimated.
Session 2 · Extraction into structured fields
Define the schema before the prompt: name the fields you need, their types, and which of them are required, instead of asking for a free-form summary.
Extract fields from a document with a vision model and validate the result against that schema, checking types, ranges, dates, totals, and required values.
Ground every extracted field in its source by returning the page number or region it came from, so a human can verify a value without rereading the document.
Recognize document-specific failure modes: invented values in blank cells, rows merged across page breaks, confused units or currencies, and misread handwriting.
Route low-confidence extractions to human review rather than letting an uncertain value flow silently into a database.
Session 3 · Visual RAG
Understand what changes when the retrieval unit is a page image or a region of a page rather than a chunk of text, and how embeddings are produced for it.
Build hybrid retrieval that searches extracted text and page images together, and recognize which of the two wins for a given question.
Cite the page or region an answer came from, so the reader can open the document at the right place and check it.
Compare a visual pipeline with a plain text pipeline on cost, latency, and accuracy, and understand why clean digital text rarely needs page images at all.
Understand the practical cost of visual RAG: page images consume far more tokens than the equivalent text, which changes both latency and price per query.
Try: Take one scanned invoice and one clean digital PDF, run both through a text pipeline and a vision pipeline, and compare accuracy, cost, and latency.
Mental model: Document → Pages → Text layer + page images → Layout & regions → Structured fields or retrievable units
Outcome: Build a document pipeline that reads real-world PDFs and scans, extracts validated fields, and answers questions with a citation back to the page it used.

M12.5 · Vision: image & video generation — (sessions: 2)

Session 1 · How generation differs from prediction
Understand that a generative image model starts from random noise and repeatedly removes it, rather than predicting the next token the way an LLM does.
Understand what a prompt actually controls in image generation: subject, composition, style, and detail, with far less literal instruction-following than a text model offers.
Understand seeds and reproducibility: the same prompt with the same seed returns the same image, while a new seed produces a different interpretation of the same words.
Understand how resolution, aspect ratio, and step count affect quality, generation time, and cost.
Session 2 · Control, video and rights
Distinguish text-to-image from image-to-image, inpainting, and outpainting, and recognize which one a given editing task actually needs.
Understand reference and style conditioning, along with structural control such as pose, depth, or edge guidance, for when a prompt alone cannot hold a layout steady.
Build intuition for iteration cost: generation is cheap per image but expensive per accepted image, because most candidates are discarded.
Understand what video generation adds: time, motion, and consistency across frames, which makes length, cost, and failure modes grow quickly.
Understand provenance and rights: watermarking, content credentials, training-data licensing, and the commercial-use terms attached to generated assets.
Try: Generate the same prompt at three seeds and three aspect ratios, then edit the best result with inpainting instead of re-prompting it.
Mental model: Noise → repeated denoising guided by the prompt → image
Outcome: Produce and iteratively control generated images, and judge whether a generation task is worth its cost in review time and rights risk.

M12.6 · Audio: how sound becomes tokens — (sessions: 1)

Understand the path from a waveform to model input: audio is sampled, converted into a spectrogram or learned audio tokens, and then treated as another token stream.
Build intuition for audio cost and latency, where tokens accumulate per second of duration rather than per word, so silence and background noise are paid for too.
Distinguish transcribe-then-prompt pipelines, where a speech-to-text model produces text for an LLM, from audio-native models that consume the sound itself.
Understand what a transcript discards: tone, emphasis, hesitation, overlapping speakers, emotion, and everything that separates "fine" said warmly from "fine" said flatly.
Recognize why audio input and output are latency-sensitive, since a conversation is judged on response delay in a way that a document summary never is.
Understand common audio failure modes such as accented speech, code-switching between languages, domain jargon, poor microphones, and long silences.
Try: Run the same 30-second clip through a speech-to-text model and through an audio-native model, and ask both what the speaker's mood was.
Outcome: Choose between a transcription pipeline and an audio-native model for a given task, and explain what each choice costs you in fidelity, latency, and money.

M12.7 · Audio: speech to text & text to speech — (sessions: 2)

Session 1 · Speech to text
Understand what a speech-to-text model such as Whisper produces: a transcript, optional word timestamps, and confidence signals rather than clean edited prose.
Distinguish batch transcription of a complete recording from streaming transcription, where partial text is emitted while the speaker is still talking.
Understand what degrades transcription in practice — accents, code-mixed Hindi-English speech, background noise, and poor microphones — and supply domain vocabulary as hints.
Use speaker diarization to attribute each segment to a speaker, and understand why it is a separate step that fails in its own ways.
Read word error rate as a measure of transcription quality, and reason about which errors actually matter for whatever happens downstream.
Session 2 · Text to speech
Understand what a text-to-speech model such as Kokoro turns text into: sampled audio in a chosen voice, produced in one pass or streamed as it is generated.
Choose a voice and shape prosody through pace, pauses, and emphasis, and understand how much control a given model actually exposes.
Control the pronunciation of names, acronyms, numbers, and code-mixed text, since these are where synthetic speech usually embarrasses itself.
Stream synthesis so the first audio reaches the listener while the rest is still being generated, instead of waiting for the whole utterance.
Compare hosted and locally run speech models on cost, latency, privacy, and voice quality, and choose per workload rather than by default.
Try: Transcribe a five-minute recording that mixes Hindi and English, then read the transcript back with a synthetic voice and note where each step breaks.
Mental model: Audio → Transcript + timestamps + speakers → Text → Synthesized audio
Outcome: Build a speech pipeline that transcribes real recordings and speaks generated text back, with a clear view of where accuracy and latency are lost.

M12.8 · Audio: realtime voice agents — (sessions: 3)

Session 1 · The latency budget
Understand why a voice conversation feels broken above roughly a second of silence, and treat end-to-end latency as the primary design constraint.
Break the latency budget into its parts: capture, network, transcription, model thinking, synthesis, and playback.
Distinguish a cascaded pipeline, where speech-to-text, an LLM, and text-to-speech run in sequence, from a speech-to-speech realtime API that takes audio in and returns audio.
Compare the two architectures on latency, control, cost, debuggability, and how much of the conversation you can inspect afterwards.
Understand which parts can overlap: synthesis can begin before the model has finished, and transcription can run while the user is still speaking.
Session 2 · Turn-taking
Understand voice activity detection and endpointing as the mechanism that decides when the user has actually stopped speaking.
Recognize why endpointing is hard: pauses for thought, slow speakers, and background chatter all look like the end of a turn.
Handle barge-in, where the user interrupts mid-answer, by stopping playback and discarding the rest of the planned response.
Use partial transcripts to start work early, and understand the risk of acting on text the user has not finished saying.
Keep conversation state consistent when a turn is cut short, so the model does not believe it said something the user never heard.
Session 3 · Failure, noise & tools
Handle silence, dead air, and network loss with holding phrases, reconnection, and a graceful exit rather than an unexplained pause.
Understand what background noise, crosstalk, and poor microphones do to a live pipeline, and where to place the safeguards.
Keep the conversation alive while a tool call runs, since a lookup that takes three seconds is silence the user will not tolerate.
Decide what the agent says while it waits, and what it does when the tool fails or returns nothing useful.
Capture what a voice session needs for later reconstruction: audio, transcript, timings, and turn boundaries.
Try: Build a small voice loop with speech-to-text, an LLM, and text-to-speech, then measure the delay from the end of your sentence to the first sound of the reply.
Mental model: User speech → VAD & endpointing → Transcript → Model → Speech, with barge-in resetting the loop
Outcome: Ship a voice agent that holds a natural conversation: it knows when you stopped talking, it can be interrupted, and it keeps silence short enough to feel human.

M12.9 · Cross-modal search & retrieval — (sessions: 1)

Understand a shared embedding space, where an image and a caption that describe the same thing land close together as vectors.
Understand CLIP-style joint embeddings, where two encoders are trained so that matching image-text pairs are pulled together and mismatched pairs pushed apart.
Search images with a text query and search text with an image query, using the same vector-search machinery Level 4 introduced for text.
Distinguish cross-modal retrieval from captioning every image first and searching the captions, and recognize when each approach wins.
Recognize where cross-modal retrieval fails: fine-grained detail, text inside images, counting, and domain-specific imagery the encoder never saw.
Evaluate retrieval quality on your own images and queries rather than trusting a benchmark score measured on a general image collection.
Try: Build a small image index, query it in plain language, and compare the results with a caption-then-search baseline over the same images.
Mental model: Different modalities → one shared vector space → distance means similarity
Outcome: Decide when a task needs cross-modal retrieval and build a small image-and-text search over your own collection.

M12.10 · Choosing a multimodal model — (sessions: 1)

Start from the modalities themselves: list what must go in, whether text, images, audio, video, or documents, and what must come out.
Understand how multimodal pricing works, since per-image and per-audio-minute charges sit alongside per-token pricing and cost stops being about text length alone.
Understand the context cost of images, where a single image consumes a large, resolution-dependent share of the context window and limits how many you can pass at once.
Compare latency expectations by modality, including time to first audio for streaming voice, where a delay is heard by the user rather than merely measured.
Weigh API access against self-hosting for vision and speech, where open models are strong and per-minute costs at volume can outgrow the operational effort.
Treat privacy as a harder constraint here, because voices, faces, and scanned documents carry consent and data-residency obligations that plain text often does not.
Consider managed speech, vision, and document services from the cloud platforms alongside general multimodal models, since a narrow managed service is often cheaper and more accurate at one job.
Shortlist two or three candidates, then test them on your own images, recordings, and documents before committing, exactly as you would for a text model.
Outcome: Choose a model for a multimodal task on modality support, cost per unit, latency, and privacy, verified against your own examples.

M12.11 · Deep research & long-horizon scenarios — (sessions: 2)

Session 1 · Planning a long run
Understand what changes when a task runs for many minutes across many sources instead of resolving inside a single prompt and a single response.
Decompose a research question into sub-questions that can be investigated independently, and recognize when a question is too vague to decompose yet.
Search broadly before going deep: gather many candidate sources first, judge which few are worth reading closely, and discard the rest deliberately.
Read sources critically rather than accumulating them, distinguishing primary evidence, secondary commentary, marketing material, and outdated claims.
Reconcile sources that disagree by preferring better evidence, stating the disagreement explicitly, or reporting genuine uncertainty instead of averaging the answers.
Session 2 · Finishing a long run
Keep every claim attached to the source it came from, so a reader can verify any individual statement without repeating the whole search.
Manage a run that outlives its context by writing notes and interim summaries as the work proceeds, and by carrying forward findings rather than raw source text.
Recognize the stopping conditions of a research run: the question is answered, the sources have started repeating, or the remaining uncertainty cannot be resolved by more searching.
Produce a structured report a human can audit, with the question, the answer, the supporting evidence, the contradictions found, and the gaps that remain.
Understand the characteristic failures of long-horizon runs: drifting away from the original question, confident synthesis of thin evidence, and citations that do not support the sentence attached to them.
Try: Run the same research question twice, once with a five-minute budget and once with a thirty-minute budget, and compare what the extra time actually bought.
Outcome: Run a long-horizon research task that produces a cited, structured report whose claims a reader can check individually.

M12.12 · On-device & edge modalities — (sessions: 1)

Understand why speech and vision suit on-device execution, since the models are small, the input is private, and the network round trip is often the slowest part.
Understand quantization as trading numerical precision for size and speed, and recognize the tasks where that trade shows up first as degraded quality.
Build intuition for hardware limits on phones and laptops, including memory ceilings, thermal throttling, and battery cost during sustained inference.
Understand what on-device execution buys you: offline operation, no per-request cost, predictable latency, and data that never leaves the user's machine.
Choose deliberately between on-device, self-hosted, and API execution for each modality, since one application can reasonably use all three at once.
Try: Run a small quantized speech-to-text model locally and compare its accuracy, latency, and cost against a hosted API on the same audio.
Outcome: Decide which parts of a multimodal system belong on the user's device and accept the quality trade knowingly.

M12.13 · Capstone: ship a multimodal build — (sessions: 1)

Choose one realistic build and take it all the way: a voice agent, a document-understanding pipeline, a visual search tool, or a research-report generator.
Combine at least two modalities in a single application so the build exercises a real cross-modal boundary rather than one model in isolation.
Feed the system real inputs from your own domain, including the messy scans, accented speech, and awkward layouts that curated sample files never contain.
Handle the modality-specific failures your build will actually hit, such as unreadable regions, silence and background noise, hallucinated chart values, or ambiguous references.
Measure latency and cost per request, and report them broken down by modality so the expensive part of the pipeline is visible.
Document one failure you found by testing on real inputs, explaining what broke, how you detected it, and what you changed in response.
Package the build so another person can run it from a README with their own inputs, their own credentials, and no help from you.
Final result: a working multimodal application that survives real inputs, whose latency and cost you can quote, and whose limitations you can state precisely.

Level 12 completion outcome

By the end of this level, you should be able to:

Explain how a multimodal model represents images, audio, and text in a single sequence, and why that makes cross-modal reasoning possible.
Predict the token cost of an image, a page, or a minute of audio, and estimate the cost of a multimodal request before sending it.
Choose sensible resolution, cropping, and sampling settings instead of sending every input at maximum fidelity.
Judge which vision tasks a model handles reliably and which ones need verification, structure, or a different tool.
Extract structured data from documents, charts, and tables, and build retrieval over pages that text extraction alone would destroy.
Use image and video generation models with enough control to produce specific outputs rather than plausible ones.
Build a speech-to-text pipeline and a text-to-speech pipeline, and reason about accuracy, latency, and cost across the options available.
Build a realtime voice agent with acceptable turn-taking, interruption handling, and end-to-end latency.
Retrieve across modalities using shared embedding spaces, and understand where cross-modal similarity breaks down.
Select a model per modality on capability, latency, cost, context, and deployment rather than on a single leaderboard position.
Run a long-horizon research task that produces a cited, auditable report instead of a confident summary.
Reason about running models locally, including quantization, memory limits, and the quality you trade away for privacy and offline operation.
Ship a multimodal application that handles real inputs, whose cost and latency you have measured and whose failure modes you can name.
