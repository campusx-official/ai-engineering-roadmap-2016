Level 1 · LLM 101

Goal: Build a strong mental model of how LLMs work, why they behave the way they do, and how to choose and use the right model for a task.
Prerequisites: None · Depth: Conceptual + hands-on; no research papers or heavy mathematics · Suggested duration: 15 sessions.

M1.1 · What an LLM Actually Is — sessions: 1
Understand what a Large Language Model actually does, with next-token prediction as the core idea and generation as a repeated predict → choose → append → repeat process.
Understand what an LLM is not: a database, search engine, deterministic program, or repository of stored answers.
Understand why the same underlying model can perform writing, summarization, translation, coding, question answering, and classification.
Build intuition for how capability improves through better data, more compute, larger models, and better training.
Try: Give the same prompt multiple times and observe variation; predict the next word/token in simple sentences before asking the model.
Outcome: Explain an LLM simply: an LLM generates text by repeatedly predicting the most appropriate next token given the tokens it has already seen.
M1.2 · Tokenization — sessions: 1
Distinguish characters, words, and tokens; understand token IDs, why LLMs operate on tokens, and why different tokenizers can split the same text differently.
Explore tokenization of normal English, uncommon words, numbers, code, emojis, Hindi/Devanagari, and mixed Hindi-English text.
Connect tokenization to API pricing, context-window consumption, output length, latency, spelling difficulties, character-counting failures, and mathematical/numerical behavior.
Try: Compare token counts for an English paragraph, its Hindi translation, source code, and a list of numbers.
Outcome: Explain why two texts with roughly the same number of words can have very different token counts and LLM costs.
M1.3 · Inside an LLM: Embeddings, Attention & Transformers — sessions: 3
Session 1 · Tokens → Vectors
Understand how token IDs become embeddings, what vectors represent conceptually, why concepts are represented numerically, and how representations change as they pass through transformer layers.
Understand what model parameters are and what sizes such as 8B, 70B, and 400B+ mean.
Understand why more parameters can increase capacity, while larger does not automatically mean better.
Session 2 · Attention
Understand attention through the intuition: a token looks at other relevant tokens in the context to build a better representation of what it means.
Build a high-level understanding of queries, keys, values, attention scores, transformer layers, and causal attention — without matrix mathematics.
Session 3 · Dense vs Mixture-of-Experts
Understand a dense model, where every parameter takes part in producing every token.
Understand a Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model, where the feed-forward layers are split into many experts and a router selects only a few of them for each token.
Distinguish total parameters from active parameters, and understand why an MoE model with a very large total size can cost far less to run than a dense model of the same size.
Understand what MoE changes in practice: every expert still has to be held in memory, while the compute spent per token stays closer to that of a much smaller model.
Read MoE descriptions on model cards, such as "8x7B" or "total vs active parameters", and know what they tell you about speed, cost, and hosting requirements.
Try: Inspect an attention visualization for a sentence such as “The animal didn't cross the street because it was tired” and reason about what "it" should attend to.
Mental model: Text → Tokens → Embeddings → Transformer layers + attention → Next-token probabilities
Outcome: Explain conceptually what happens between a token entering the model and the model producing next-token probabilities.
M1.4 · How LLMs Learn — sessions: 2
Session 1 · Pretraining
Understand pretraining, how enormous text datasets are used, why next-token prediction creates its own training signal, and what self-supervised learning means.
Understand how pretraining enables models to learn grammar, facts, patterns, concepts, relationships, coding syntax, and problem-solving patterns.
Explore memorization vs generalization, including why an LLM can sometimes answer questions it has never seen verbatim.
Session 2 · Post-training
Understand the broad pipeline: Pretraining → Supervised Fine-Tuning → Preference / Reinforcement Training → Deployed Assistant.
Understand SFT, RLHF, RLAIF, preference optimization at a conceptual level, and safety tuning.
Connect post-training to why chat models follow instructions, answer conversationally, refuse certain requests, hedge, structure answers, and ask for clarification.
Try: Compare a base model and an instruction-tuned model using identical prompts.
Outcome: Explain why a pretrained model may know a lot while still being a poor conversational assistant.
M1.5 · How LLM Inference Works — sessions: 1
Understand what happens after sending a prompt to an LLM API: Prompt → process input → predict token → generate token → repeat.
Distinguish prefill, where the model processes the input/context, from decode, where new tokens are generated one by one.
Understand input tokens, output tokens, time to first token (TTFT), tokens per second, and total latency.
Build intuition for KV caching and why the model does not need to recompute the entire conversation from scratch after every generated token.
Connect inference mechanics to latency, cost, long prompts, long outputs, and reasoning models.
Outcome: Explain why generating 2,000 output tokens usually takes much longer than generating 50 tokens even with the same input.
M1.6 · The Context Window — sessions: 1
Understand what the context window is and what can occupy it: system instructions, conversation history, user prompts, documents, examples, tool outputs, and generated content.
Distinguish context ≠ model knowledge ≠ long-term memory.
Understand what can happen with very long contexts: truncation, attention degradation, lost-in-the-middle effects, and context dilution.
Understand why a model supporting a huge context window does not mean it can use every token equally well.
Try: Place an important fact near the beginning, middle, and end of a long document and compare retrieval performance.
Outcome: Decide whether a task needs a larger-context model or a better strategy for selecting what enters context.
M1.7 · Sampling & Generation Parameters — sessions: 1
Start from the model's next-token probability distribution and ask: which token should actually be generated?
Understand greedy decoding, temperature, top-p, top-k, maximum output tokens, stop sequences, and repetition/frequency/presence penalties where supported.
Understand task-dependent randomness: lower randomness for extraction, structured output, classification, and factual QA; higher randomness for brainstorming, creative writing, and diverse alternatives.
Understand that different model APIs expose different controls and that some reasoning models provide less direct control over traditional sampling parameters.
Try: Run the same prompt at several temperature settings and compare consistency, creativity, and correctness.
Outcome: Choose sensible generation settings based on the task instead of changing temperature arbitrarily.
M1.8 · Reasoning Models & Test-Time Compute — sessions: 1
Understand why some models spend substantially more compute before producing an answer.
Build intuition for test-time compute, reasoning effort, thinking budgets, and internal reasoning tokens/compute.
Identify tasks where reasoning tends to help: difficult mathematics, complex coding, planning, multi-step logic, and constraint-heavy problems.
Identify tasks where it may be unnecessary: sentiment classification, simple extraction, rewriting, and straightforward summarization.
Understand the trade-off: more reasoning can improve quality but generally increases cost and latency.
Adapt prompting for reasoning models: move away from relying on “think step by step” and instead clearly state the goal, provide relevant context, specify constraints, and define what a good answer looks like.
Try: Run the same problems through a fast general model and a reasoning model; compare accuracy, latency, and cost.
Outcome: Recognize when paying for extra reasoning compute is likely to be worthwhile.
M1.9 · LLM Capabilities & Failure Modes — sessions: 1
Understand common strengths: summarization, text transformation, extraction, classification, semantic understanding, coding, translation, brainstorming, working with unstructured information, and few-shot adaptation.
Understand common failure modes: hallucination, arithmetic mistakes, brittle reasoning, instruction-following failures, prompt sensitivity, context failures, outdated knowledge, nondeterminism, and confident incorrect answers.
Understand hallucination mechanistically: an LLM is trained to generate a likely continuation, not to independently verify that every generated statement is true, which makes fluent incorrect answers possible.
Adopt the engineering question: instead of “Can GPT do this?”, ask “How reliably does this model perform this task under the conditions of my application?”
Try: Create 10 questions where the model knows the answer, probably does not know it, receives misleading information, or receives insufficient information; observe how confidence changes.
Outcome: Predict which tasks are naturally well suited to LLMs and which require additional safeguards.
M1.10 · Understanding the Model Landscape — sessions: 1
Understand closed/API models through trade-offs involving frontier capability, convenience, infrastructure, privacy, vendor dependence, and control.
Understand open-weight models through self-hosting, privacy, customization, infrastructure requirements, and operational complexity.
Learn other useful distinctions: small vs large, dense vs Mixture-of-Experts, general-purpose vs specialized, reasoning vs non-reasoning, and text-only vs multimodal.
Internalize the core principle: there is no universally best LLM; the best model depends on the workload.
Understand that a model's knowledge stops at its training cutoff, and that anything newer has to arrive through context, retrieval, or tools.
Track model versions and dated snapshots, and expect deprecations: the model behind a familiar name can change or be retired, so pin a version and re-test when it moves.
Check the license before building on an open-weight model, since permissive, restricted-use, and non-commercial terms differ in what you are allowed to ship.
Outcome: Compare two models using meaningful architectural, capability, and deployment differences rather than relying purely on leaderboard rank.
M1.11 · Benchmarks & Leaderboards — sessions: 1
Understand what a benchmark is: a fixed set of tasks with a fixed scoring method, used to measure different models against the same yardstick.
Recognize the benchmarks quoted on model cards — knowledge and reasoning tests such as MMLU and GPQA, coding tests, and mathematics tests — and know roughly what each one measures.
Read the main kinds of leaderboard: human-preference rankings such as LMArena/Chatbot Arena, aggregated capability/cost/latency dashboards, and open-model leaderboards.
Understand why a leaderboard rank rarely transfers to your application: different prompts, different tasks, averaged scores, and someone else's definition of a good answer.
Understand the pitfalls that inflate published scores: data contamination, saturated benchmarks, overfitting to public test sets, and favorable run configurations.
Use benchmarks and leaderboards for the one thing they do well — narrowing a crowded field to a shortlist — and then judge the shortlist on your own examples.
Try: Shortlist three models from a leaderboard for a task you care about, then compare their published scores against how they perform on ten of your own examples.
Outcome: Read benchmark tables and leaderboards without being misled by them, and turn them into a shortlist rather than a decision.

M1.12 · Choosing the Right Model — sessions: 1

Evaluate models across eight dimensions:

Capability: Can it perform the task at all?
Quality: How reliably does it perform your specific task?
Latency: How quickly does it respond?
Cost: What will each request cost at your expected usage?
Context: Can it accommodate the required input?
Modalities: Do you need text, images, audio, or video?
Privacy & deployment: Can you use an external API, or do you require self-hosting?
Product features: Do you require structured outputs, function calling, tools, streaming, or caching?
Learn to critically read model cards, benchmark tables, pricing pages, context-window specifications, and API documentation.
Understand why benchmark scores alone should not determine model selection.
Follow the workflow: Define task → Identify requirements → Shortlist models → Test on your own examples → Measure quality → Measure latency → Measure cost → Inspect failures → Choose the model.
Outcome: Select models based on application requirements and empirical performance rather than popularity or leaderboard position.
